Ring Keepers

Saturn
The Ring Keepers gather ice on the cloud tops beneath the rings of Saturn. The rings — the Crown, as they call them — shed ice without end, and the keepers catch it in nets before it sinks into the Undercloud, live on floes of it and dream of one day giving it back. Every keeper wears a little ring of ice around the head that widens with the years, and greets a bare-headed guest from Earth — the Blue Grain — like a child before its first catch.

Where they live

Ring Keepers' Floes. A village of the Ring Keepers: domes made in the planet's own image, banded cloud bound with frost and girdled by a ring, each on its own floe of fallen ring ice and each under a net that catches what the rings let fall. In the middle stands an obelisk cut from the first ice the founders caught, with a ring of its own, and the elder stands beside it.

What to talk about

Tell me about Saturn.

There is no ground here, guest, not anywhere. Below the clouds are more clouds, then haze, then the Undercloud, and nothing that falls there comes back. Above us is the Crown. We live in between, on what the Crown drops. Our world is so light, the old songs say, that it would float in water — if anyone ever found that much water.

What is down in the Undercloud?

Warmth, strangely. Deep down helium falls as rain, and the falling rain gives off heat — that is why our world is warmer than the Far Fire could ever make it. But with the warmth comes a weight that crushes ice into nothing. A floe that sinks is a floe gone. We do not go down. We only listen.

How do you bear this cold?

We were born in it, and our skin keeps the little warmth that rises from the depths. You were not — I can see your shell steaming. The helium distillers sell heaters for shells like yours: the warmth of the helium rain, in a box. Buy one before your fingers stop obeying you.

Why does the wind turn so sharply?

Around the pole the wind runs along six straight sides, each wider than your whole world, and turns at every corner as if it had hit a wall. When that wind reaches the floes, hold the rail and give it your back — at the corner it will turn you round anyway.

Who else lives on Saturn?

The gliders are our little cousins: they ride the same updrafts and sleep in our nets, and we never chase them out. The frost kites circle the hexagon for days and strike only those who strike first. And under the floes swim the sharks, barred like amber and ice. They circle below and strike upward. Never dangle your feet over the edge.

Who are you?

We are those who catch what the Crown lets fall. Every family has its floe, every floe has its net, and every net has a catcher who watches the sky. We are not many and we do not hurry. The Crown taught us patience: it drops a grain at a time.

What is the ring around your head?

My circlet. When a child catches its first grain of ice, the grain is not put away: it starts to circle the child's head, and every catch after that adds a little to it. That is why the old have wide rings and the young narrow ones. When one of us is gone, the circlet is let go. It flies up, they say, back to the Crown.

Could I have a ring of my own?

Your own? You would have to catch your first grain, guest, and then wait a long turn or two. But here — this is dust of the Crown, from the last generous rain. Keep it: it is what a circlet is made of. Maybe yours will start with this.

Why do you live on floes?

Because a cloud holds you only until you crouch, and a house cannot stand on a cloud at all. A floe is fallen ice planed flat and paved with frost. But a floe is always sinking, a little every turn. Feed it new ice and it floats; forget it for a long turn and the Undercloud takes it. So we catch. Catching is how we stay up.

What is the obelisk in the middle?

The first ice. When our founders came to these clouds, the first block they caught was not used for anything: they stood it on end and put a ring around it, so that the floes would always remember what they are made of. Every village has one. Ours still glows at night — there is light frozen inside.

Why did they let go?

Every child asks that, and every elder answers the same way: you let go of what you hold when you need both hands for something else. The Watchers wrote that the Crown dropped its first grain on the day a bright spark went out in the north of the sky. Which spark, they did not write. Something that needed both hands. And so the answer comes back round to the question.

How many rings did they set?

Sixteen, the old count says. The slab in the Ice Cathedral says eight of them went down — to places with no sky at all — and only eight stand under a sky like ours. We have counted it many times, guest, and we do not like the sum. A ring that goes down is a ring that falls, and nobody is down there with a net.

The tablet from the Spring says: the voice does not sleep.

Of course it does not. The Spring has never stopped once: every turn it throws ice into the outer ring — the one gift that still goes up instead of falling down. We always thought the Spring was the last hand of the Crown-Holders, still laying grain. If it is their voice as well... then they are not only giving, guest. They are calling. And a call comes back round too.

What is the Crown?

The rings, in your words. Ice, almost all of it — grains like sand and blocks the size of floes — spread so thin that ten of our steps would cross it, and so wide that twenty worlds like yours could lie side by side on it. The grandmothers said the world has not worn it for long: it is a young gift. Little moons graze along its edges — the Shepherds — and keep it from spilling.
