Windbearers

Neptune
Tall, streamlined people of the fastest winds in the Solar System. Everything on Neptune that is neither tied down nor heavy is carried away, so the Windbearers are both: stone weights on their feet, a line at the belt, an anchor under every house, and the heavier one's weights, the more the village listens. To them the wind is the speech of the world, and a guest without weights is a "lightweight" — welcome, but in need of something to hold on to.

Where they live

Windbearers' Anchorage. An anchorage of the Windbearers: squat houses of dense stone with walls two stones thick, their low backs turned to the open cloud and their tall doorways to the lanes, every roof held down by ropes with stones hanging from both ends. Each slab of the anchorage rests on an anchor stone and hangs weights into the cloud, the way its people wear weights on their feet. In the middle stands the First Stone with the village lyre, and the elder stands beside it.

What to talk about

Tell me about Neptune.

There is no ground here, lightweight. Only cloud on cloud, floor below floor, and wind through all of them. The wind never stops — it only gets louder or quieter. The Far Lamp gives almost no warmth, so the cold is everywhere. What is heavy stays. What is light goes. That is all of Neptune.

Why does the wind never stop?

Because the world is warm inside. The Far Lamp gives us little, but Neptune breathes out more heat than it gets — from its belly, from the Bottom. The warm rises, the cold falls, and the sky runs around the world faster than anything. We say the wind is the breath of the world. Every voice we have ever let go travels in it.

What is beneath the clouds?

More clouds. We live on the top floor, where the white cloud is firm enough to hold a stone. Below it is the eels' road, a long fall through empty air. Then the dark floor, indigo and cobalt, where black stones grow. Then the haze, thick as a blanket. And under everything lies the Bottom: they say the sky is pressed so hard there that it rains diamonds. No diver has reached it. The deeper they go, the warmer it gets.

Which moon walks backwards across the sky?

The Oncoming One. Everything in our sky runs with the wind; it alone walks against it. Only something very heavy can do that, so the Oncoming One is the eldest of all weights. But even it is losing: every year it comes a little closer. Our harpists say that one day it will fall, break apart and lie around the world as a ring. Not soon. Not in the life of anyone's grandchildren.

Who else lives on Neptune?

The lanterns drift in flocks above the cloud — little bells of cold light. They harm no one, and our divers carry their gel down into the dark. The gale eels ride the fast wind on their sails; leave them be, their flanks bite with lightning, but their fins make the best kites. And the maws... the maws come up from under the Mouth. Never follow a light at night that is not one of ours.

How do you bear this cold?

We were born in it: the cold is our weather, not our enemy. But you shiver even inside your shell, lightweight, I can see it. Here — warmroots. They reach down toward the warm Bottom and keep its heat inside. Eat one when the cold gets into you.

Who are you?

We are the ones who hold on. The wind of Neptune takes everything that is not tied down or heavy — so we are both. Stone on our feet, a line at the belt, an anchor under every house. Our grandmothers' grandmothers learned it before anything else: first hold on, then live.

Why do you wear stones on your feet?

Without them the first gust would carry me over the edge of the cloud. But weights are more than that. A child gets its first pebble on the day it stands up, and every good deed adds a stone: a line well tied, a diver pulled back, a storm sat through. The heavier the weights, the more the village listens. Look at the elder's boots — half a block of stone each. Nobody argues with that.

Could I have weights of my own?

Your boots would not hold real weights — they are soft, like the rest of you. But take this: the light-stone from the toe of my old weights. It is superionic ice, the heaviest thing that glows. Carry it in your pocket, and the wind will know you belong somewhere.

What do you do when one of you dies?

We untie them. The family takes off the weights, stone by stone, carries the body to the edge of the cloud and lets go. The wind takes them gently: it always knew they were coming. That is why we listen to the wind — everyone we have ever let go speaks in it. The stones stay with the family. My boots hold my mother's last stone.

Why is everything here tied down?

Because everything here wants to leave. Houses, pots, children, the elder's stories — all of it would go downwind if we let it. So every house is tied to anchor stones, every one of us wears a line at the belt, and every knot is a promise. When a braider ties you in, they say: "I will not let go." When the wind is loud, we clip our lines to the rings in the walls and wait.

What do you call my world?

The Calm. The guests say that on your world the wind lies down and sleeps for whole days: nothing blows away, and you do not even tie your houses. We laughed when we first heard it. And here is a stranger thing: your grandmothers found Neptune before they ever saw it — they felt our weight pulling at our neighbor. You found us by our weight, lightweight. That is the right way to find anyone.

What do your people do?

Haulers drag the dense stone and set the anchors — you know them by the anchor on the back. Harpists listen to the wind and tell us what it says. Braiders twist the lines that keep us here. Divers go down into the dark for black stones, and not all of them come back. Storm catchers fly kites into the high wind and bring down its sparks. And the elder holds it all together by being heavier than all of it.

What does the wind say?

Everything, if you know how to listen. It says when a storm is coming and from where; it says when the Mouth wakes; it says who is walking on the cloud before you see them. It speaks with the voices of everyone we have let go. Lightweights hear only noise. That is not the wind's fault.
