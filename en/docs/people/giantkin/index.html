Giantkin

The Ash
A stocky gray folk of the Ash with bone plates on their skulls and shoulders and blue fire in their eyes. They call the giants their Giants: the giants burned down, and the blue fire is what is left of their souls. The Giantkin keep that fire, roll warm felt from the down of ember mice, guard the bones from the gnawers and say that warmth is given, never taken.

Where they live

Bonefold Camp. A camp of the Giantkin: felt yurts under ribs that fell from the Giants of their own accord, homes in a skull and a vertebra, a skull of an ancestor on a pole by every family door and a blue fire in every hearth. In the middle burns the kin fire under four ribs that do not meet, and the elder lives in a Giant's skull whose eyes still burn. Past the last yurt the path ends at a Bone Gate for those who leave.

What to talk about

Tell me about the Ash.

The Ash is the bottom of the world's hearth. Once the Giants burned here, and it was warm everywhere. Now there is soul ash to the edge of sight, the Giants lying where they fell, and blue fire that gives no warmth. We live between the bones and keep what is left.

What is the dark hole in the sky?

The Chimney. The Giants were the hearth, and that is its flue: all the warmth went up through it long ago, to where you came from. You came down it in your bell, on a chain. Sometimes warm air still sinks back down from it, and then the mice come out and we take off three layers.

Why is it so cold here?

Because the fire here is itself cold. A Giant's fire has lost its body; it reaches for warmth the way we reach for fire. Stand by it, and it drinks from you. Felt, a smoldering tuber in the belly, a friend's back against yours — that is how one keeps warm in the Ash. The heater in your suit helps too, I hear.

What falls from the sky here?

Ash snow: the Giants shaking out their pipes, and it is colder while it falls. Bone dust: the wind grinding the fallen ones into flour; the Ash goes white, you cannot see the next rib, and the guides ring their bells so that nobody walks off. And sometimes the warm haze comes down the Chimney. That is the best: the ember mice run out of their burrows, and the children chase them.

Who else lives in the Ash?

The ember mice, warm and kind. The gnawers, who crack the Giants for their marrow. And the strays — pieces of fire in hoods of ash. Respect the mice, chase the gnawers, and never turn your back on a stray.

Tell me about the gnawers.

Thieves of marrow. They wear other creatures' bones grown into their hides, and they crack the fallen Giants for the blue marrow — that is why their jaws glow. A fed gnawer leaves you be; strike one, and the whole pack answers. We drive them off the bones with fire on poles. Their fangs make the best chisels: the carvers will buy every one you bring.

And the little ones with fire on their tails?

Ember mice! The only warm thing in the Ash that is not a warmling. They sleep in heaps under the ribs, warming one another, the way we sleep. They leave their down at the mouths of their burrows, and we roll our felt from it. Do not hurt them. Well — if you already have, the felt maker will still take the down.

What is worth seeing in the Ash?

Everything here is either a Giant, or something we built for the Giants, or something strangers left behind. Ask about any of it.

I saw a skull as big as a hill.

A Giant's skull. They are rare: whatever took the Giants took their heads first. It lies on the back of its head and looks up into the Chimney, and one eye socket still burns. We go there to listen. Put your ear to the bone, and you will hear him thinking — slowly, about warm things.

And the ring of benches around a blue fire?

The Soul Pyre. The keepers bring it the embers we win back from the strays, and the fire takes them home. The skulls on the benches are ours: kin who sat with the fire so that it would not be lonely, and sat too long. Nobody has ever got warm by it. That is not what it is for.

Who built the gate that leads nowhere?

We did. It is a gate for those who leave. When one of us goes cold, the family walks them to the Bone Gate, and they go through. The road stops four steps past it, because the rest of the road is only for the dead. The lanterns are lit for whoever is going.

What is the field of glowing stumps?

A cinder field. Something grew there and burned, and the coals never cooled and never warmed. We gather soul cinder there for our hearths: a Giant's fire lives long on it. The skulls at the roots belong to warmlings who took the glow for a fire and sat down to warm their hands.

And the well with the bone windlass?

The old keepers dug those, looking for warmth under the ash. They found a bottom with no bottom. We pour the ash of our hearths into it, so it can fall back down to wherever fire comes from. Do not lean over the hole: the blue fire around it is lit so the careless can see where not to step.

What lies under the long mounds?

Not ours. The barrows are older than the oldest layer on any mantle. Those who lie there wore wraith iron — iron the Giants' cold has got into — and they get up when someone comes close. We do not dig there. The sifters find crumbs of that iron in the ash, and that is near enough for us.
