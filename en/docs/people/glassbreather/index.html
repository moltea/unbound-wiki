Glassbreathers

The Breath
The people of the Breath. They breathe the lilac vapor that kills every other living thing, keep it in the glass sac at their throats until it calms, and breathe out heat: with it they melt fulgurite and blow glass. Their houses are glass bells on the islands, every one with its own voice, and the lightning season is their working season. A guest in a suit they call a little bubble: air from somewhere else, kept in glass.

Where they live

Chiming Isles. A settlement of the Glassbreathers: houses that are glass bells, each cast from fulgurite the whole family melted with its breath, each on an island shard moored to the main island by railed bridges. Lightning ribs of fulgurite run down the glass, and a glowing tongue hangs inside every bell. In the middle stands the Tower of Last Breaths, hung with little bells that keep the last breaths of the dead, and the elder stands at its foot.

What to talk about

What else do you remember of them?

Warmth. Hhh. The first Storm Lantern — not ours. Found. Already lit. And the warm hand — the steam that rises and catches you when you fall off an edge? We say that is the Hands. Remembering. A breath that big... was never cold.

Where did the Hands go?

Hhh. Into the dark. All the bells agree on that. On nothing else. One old bell says: they went to hold something else. Something you cannot breathe out. Something hungry. ...That bell is cracked. We keep it anyway. A cracked bell can still be right.

The tablet says: a warden was set by the road.

Set. Hhh. So the Hands did not only hold it. They put it there. At the door. A question at the door — not a lock. Whoever answers goes through. ...You went through, bubble. You answered the One Who Breathed Us. Then one of its words is finished. Two breaths. I need two breaths. ...There.

How can you breathe the vapor?

Breathing it in is easy; keeping it is the trick. The vapor eats meat and lungs, but our sac is glass inside, and glass it eats slowest of all. We breathe in, hold it in the sac until it calms and glows, and breathe it out as heat. You would dissolve before your second breath, bubble. Do not try.

Is there any way I could take a breath here?

Not of ours. But sometimes the vapor settles around a grain of glass, layer on layer, and becomes a pearl, and a pearl, crushed, gives back clean air, the kind your shell likes. Here: a breath of the Breath for your road. Do not crush it before you need it.

Why don't the islands fall?

Because they are full of breath. Break a piece of searing glass and look: bubbles, thousands of them, each a breath the glass kept when the lightning fused it. Glass full of bubbles is light; the stone your people call pumice floats on your water the same way. Our islands float on the Breath like foam.

What is below the vapor?

The Deep: two tiers of vapor, lilac and pale, where the lambs graze and the drakes hunt. Below that is the Bottom, a dark haze where breath goes to sleep. Whatever falls there does not come back... unless the warm hand catches it. Some days the lower vapor warms and rises, and whoever slips off an edge sinks slowly, as if somebody were holding them.

Who else lives in the Breath?

The mist lambs, little clouds with hooves: we herd them. The fulgurite crawlers, lightning that grew legs: leave them be and they leave you be. And the vapor drakes, breath that grew bones of glass and remembered how to hunt. A drake drinks the air out of a shell through its gills. If you see two lights in the vapor, bubble, those are its eyes. Go under a bell.

Did the whisperers like the lamb wool?

The herder sent our lamb wool to the whisperers in the world that looks back, and they hung it at their elder's door. Since then the herder shears more carefully, as if every tuft were going to a door somewhere.

What is worth seeing on the isles?

Ask about what you have seen, and I will tell you whose it is. Some of it is ours, some the lightning made by itself, and some the sealed ones left behind.

What is the frame of bells on a shard?

The Vapor Chimes, the oldest bells we have, older than any house. There is no wind on the Breath, and still they ring, a few notes at a time, always just before the lightning. Our grandmothers' grandmothers built the frame to hear the storm coming. One bell has fallen; we leave it where it lies. A bell that fell has said its last word.

And the tower with the lantern?

The Storm Lantern. Ours too, but not for warning: for calling. When a storm comes near, the lantern lights by itself and pulls the lightning to its island, away from our bells, and the bolts fuse the ash there into fulgurite for the founders. Someone tore the railing off the gallery. Drakes, we think. They hate a light that is not theirs.

Who planted the forest of glass columns?

The lightning. Every bolt that strikes an island leaves a column of fused glass where it went in, and the sky likes some places better than others. The oldest columns are taller than a bell. Our catchers go there in the season to listen for the columns that still spark.

What is the lumpy glass that looks like coral?

A glass reef. Nobody blows it: it grows by itself where the vapor is thickest, a skin a year, around whatever it touched first, and charge glows trapped in its hollows. Our glassblowers say a reef is the Breath trying to blow its own glass, and doing it badly.
