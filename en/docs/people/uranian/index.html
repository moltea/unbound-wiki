Frostwalkers

Uranus
Gentle giants of Uranus who walk on their knuckles and count their lives in days, each day forty-two years long: the planet lies on its side, and every place on it has a Long Day and then a Long Night. Through the Day they store warmth, carve ice and catch the diamond rain; through the Night they sleep together in halls dug into the ice. Diamonds are only weather to them, and warmth is wealth, so a guest with coal in their pack is welcome at every dome.

Where they live

Frostwalker Wintering. A wintering of the Frostwalkers on a raft of ice crust among the clouds: low domes of dense snow with round door-stones, a sleep hall dug into the ice where the whole village will sleep through the forty-two-year Night, funnels on tall poles that catch the diamond rain, and stacks of glowing methane ice by every door. In the middle, beside the Cradle with her upright hoops, the elder keeps the Ember: the only orange fire on Uranus.

What to talk about

Tell me about this world.

This is the Cradle. She lies on her side, moth, and rocks: for forty-two years one end of her looks at the Far Fire, then for forty-two years the other. So every place on her has one Long Day and one Long Night, and in between, the sky blinks. We live where the heavy things settle — the diamonds, and us.

But the sky keeps going dark. Isn't that night?

That is a blink. The Cradle spins on her side, quick as a cyan puff, and the Far Fire slips behind her edge for half of every turn — seventeen of your hours, the astronomer says. You moths count every blink as a day; no wonder you are always in a hurry. We do not even look up for a blink.

Why would anyone push a world over?

My mother had another story, and I like hers better. She said that in the old days something came at the Cradle out of the dark and struck it hard, and the same day the Patchwork broke to pieces. The Pushers did not knock us over: they caught us as we fell and laid us gently on our side, the way you lay down a child who has hit its head. From below, a hand that pushes and a hand that catches look the same.

What were the Pushers like?

Warm. Every song starts with that, before tall, before strong: warm. Where they put their palms on the Cradle to lay it down, the ice melted, and the hollows are still there, full of the clearest ice we have. And the burning stone that fell in the first swing — the oldest mothers thought it fell by itself. I think somebody warm left a coal for neighbors with a long night ahead of them. That is what I would do.

The tablet from the Patchwork says: the thread holds the worlds.

Then my mother was right. She stands among the Unwoken in the yard now; I will tell her when the Day comes. The Patchwork is sewn — on a clear blink you can see the seams from here. Someone broke it, and someone sewed it, and the sewing held. That is a warm thought for a long night, little moth: somewhere there were hands for mending worlds.

Most of you?

The old ones sometimes stay asleep. Before every Night the sculptors carve each of us in ice, so that if we do not wake, our figure is still standing there in the morning. If you see ice figures in a yard, that is who they are. We call them the Unwoken, and when the Day comes, we tell them the news.

How do you bear the cold?

The Cradle is the coldest of the Far Fire's children — your astronomers measured it, they say. She has almost no fire of her own: her sisters are warm inside, and she is not. So we wear our fur thick, keep our warmth in our backs and never waste a scrap of it. Your shell is clever, moth, but it is thin.

The Windbearers say their warmth comes up from the Bottom.

The astronomer told the whole wintering, and nobody slept that blink. The deep blue cousin keeps a fire in its belly and breathes out more warmth than the Far Fire sends it — and the Cradle has none of her own. The old ones think she spilled hers long ago, the day something laid her on her side. We are not jealous, moth. A world without its own fire teaches you to keep warm together. If you go back, tell the Windbearers: we lie close, fur to fur, and that is our Bottom.

Who else lives here?

The hail-crabs are our cousins: the rain takes root on them as it does on us, only they hide under it. Leave them be; a crab's claw has opened harder things than a moth. The cyan puffs love anything new — they will love you, follow you about and steal dust from the catchers' funnels. And in the haze walk the cold breaths. Those are not alive. Stay near a lamp.

What is worth seeing around here?

Some of the Cradle's wonders we made, some the frost made, and some the moths left behind. Ask about ours, and I will tell you what the old ones told me. About the moths' things ask me separately — those make me sad.

Who stacks the ice cairns with the glowing stone?

We do, before every Night. Whoever wakes first must find the way between the halls in the dark, so we stack boulders of ice where the hail lies thickest and set the three biggest stones of the Day on top. The topmost glows and shows the way home. The children call them the sleepers' lamps.

And the arch of ice between two ridges?

The frost grew it in a single cold blink, the way it grows everything here. Our grandmothers walked across it once, in a kind year. Now a third of it has fallen, and we are too heavy to try. Someone light crossed after us and left a barrel on the far ridge. A moth, surely: nobody else is light enough to be that brave.

What is the frozen jet in the calm band?

The Fountain. Once the deep breathed out a whole column of methane, and the cold caught it before it could fall: the drops still hang in the air where they froze. The openers go there for the frozen spray — it burns long and blue. The topmost drop glows. They say a spark of the first fire is still inside it.

Who are you?

Frostwalkers. Big, slow, and warm inside, if we are careful. We walk on our knuckles because the cloud gives way under two feet, and on four it holds. We count our lives in days, moth — real days, forty-two years each — and none of us gets very many.
