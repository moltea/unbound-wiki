Comb Keepers

The Hive
Shulker kin who stayed in the Hive when the Queen closed her shell and the combs stopped flying. Every family keeps its own comb, seals its wax, trades its amber and counts its wealth in sealed cells; their shells are cells themselves, and they slam the lids shut when frightened. They greet a guest by lifting the lid like a hat, and they call humans the lidless: a head always open to the sky looks terribly brave to them.

Where they live

Keepers' Comb. A settlement of the Comb Keepers: family cells of amber wax huddled around the Queen's Court, each under a lid left ajar like a keeper's own shell, with the family's sealed cells pressed into its walls for everyone to count. In the middle of the court the Little Queen Cell hangs under an amber canopy, and the candles beneath it are never allowed to go out.

What to talk about

What were the Wing-Givers like?

Big. Kind, I think, but big. Our oldest piece of amber has a print pressed into it — a fingertip, nothing like a keeper's — and the fingertip alone is wider than a lid. The amber workers say it is theirs: the first cell of the Comb was sealed with one touch. And warm. Wax goes soft by a hearth, and the oldest wax we have is still softer than any other.

Then why did the Comb settle?

Because the Mother closed her shell. The old keepers say the hunger passed us by because the Comb is one creature with many hands, and it found nothing separate to bite. I think — keep your lid shut about it — the Mother heard it coming back. A closed shell does not shine, guest, and a Comb sitting still in the dark looks like a stone. The flight was their gift. The hiding was hers.

The tablet of Hunger tells of a swarm of mirrors around a star.

A swarm... that gathered light? Oh. Oh, the poor cells. We flew dark, and they hung there shining, catching light for someone, and the hunger came for the light. That is why the Wing-Givers gave us wings and not mirrors. There was another comb out there, guest, and nobody gave it wings.

The combs flew?

In the Flight everything floated: the combs, the wax, the amber, us. The drones carried bridges from comb to comb, and the Mother sat in the middle and hummed. Then she closed her shell, and everything came down. We call that the Settling. Sometimes the combs remember — then you feel light, and things rise out of the ground. Catch them.

What are the wells between the combs?

Cells that were never filled. Wax runs down into them from every comb around and sets at the bottom, layer on layer. The oldest layers are pressed so hard they turn to amber. The drones get angry when someone breaks a well's wall — and so do we, a little. And they are deep, guest. Very deep.

What do you call the other worlds?

Cells, of course. The Comb is one cell of a much greater comb, and the cracks you came through are cracks in its walls. Beyond them lie the Dark Cell, where our shell-cousins keep their purple towers, the Glass Cell, the Steam Cell and the Watching Cell. Yours is the Green Cell — you smell of it.

Who else lives in the Hive?

The wax grubs — little ones, they chew wax and purr; we keep them in the nurseries and scratch their caps. The drones, who fly in threes and keep the walls. Our shell-cousins, the shulkers, who never learned manners: they shoot first. And the wardens, deep down. Ask me about the drones and the wardens separately — those are long stories.

Who are you?

Keepers of the comb. When the Mother closed her shell, most of the shell-folk went through the cracks into the Dark Cell and grew wild there in their towers. We stayed. Every family keeps its own comb, seals its wax, trades its amber and remembers. Somebody has to keep the cells, guest, even if nobody is coming back to fill them.

What is your shell made of?

Wax and amber, and it is a cell itself: each of us is born in a cell and carries it all our life. The lid is my door and my hat and my mouth. When I am glad, it claps; when I am afraid, it slams, and then I sit inside until the world is quiet. If you ever hit one of us, that is what you will see — and that keeper will not speak to you for a good while.

Do you give anything to guests?

Honey. The first thing a keeper gives a guest is a cup of honey, so that the guest remembers the way back. Here, take two. Drink one when you are hurt; plant the other on a comb cap, and it will give you more.

How do you count wealth?

By sealed cells. A sealed cell holds something worth keeping — honey, amber, a memory pressed into wax. The young have open cells on their shells; the elder has every one sealed. When two keepers argue, they count each other's caps. The argument ends quickly.

What does each of you do?

The sealer wears a lid of white wax and carries a pot of hot wax on their back; the amber worker wears goggles and carries a lump of raw amber; the chorus tender grows a garden right on the lid; the cell builder wears a purple helmet and carries bricks; the drone warden has a wide hat with a drone sitting on it. And the elder has the crown and the staff with the amber sphere. You will know us from far away.

Are you kin to the shulkers?

Shell-cousins. We were one folk in the Flight. When the Comb came down, they went through the cracks into the Dark Cell, built purple towers there, and now they shoot at everything that moves. A few stayed and went wild in the nests on the combs — keep a shield ready near those. We are the ones who stayed and stayed polite.

What is chorus wax?

Chorus drinks the light of the Comb and sweats wax, and the Comb sweats it too, from every seam. Fresh wax is soft and smells of chorus; old wax runs down into the wells and sets; very old wax, pressed by the weight of the combs, becomes amber. Everything we own is wax of some age.
