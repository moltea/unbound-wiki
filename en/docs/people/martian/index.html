Martians

Mars
The people of the storms: tall, four-armed and plated in rust, with a sail along the back that feels the wind before it comes. They count time in dust storms, drink salt and iron out of stone, and hold water holy. They live in towers of red brick above the storms and call humans bareskins.

Where they live

Leeward Towers. A Martian windhold: tall narrow towers of red mud brick above the storms, cellars dug into the ground where the ice keeps, windcatchers on the roofs and covered walks between the towers. In the middle stands the storm bell tower, and its bell rings when the sails of the elders feel a storm coming.

What to talk about

Tell me about Mars.

Red ground, thin air, cold that bites at night. And wind — always wind. Once there were seas here: you can see their bones in the pale layers of the canyons, in the blue-green clay that only water makes. The seas left. The wind stayed. We stayed with the wind.

What can you see in the sky from here?

Two moons that cannot agree: the Runner rises in the west and crosses the sky twice a day, the Slowfoot dawdles in the east for days. The Water Star at dusk, small and blue. And at sunset the sky turns blue around the sun — the only blue we ever see, except in ice.

Why does the Runner hurry so?

He is falling. Slowly, the way dust settles in still air. The elders say one day he will come too close and break apart, and we will have a ring like the far giants. My grandchildren's grandchildren will count their storms by it.

Who else lives on Mars?

Rollers are gentle and plated; in a storm they curl into balls and let the wind roll them down the canyons. We herd them for the plates they shed. Dust devils are children of the storm, a black stone heart in a coat of wind; leave them alone and they leave you. And the rust scorpions under the dunes. Those you do not leave alone. You leave.

What is worth seeing around here?

The wind carved most of it, our ancestors some of it, and the bareskins left the rest. Walk with the wind and you will find all three. Ask about any, and I will tell you what the towers remember.

I have read a tablet of those who set the rings. Whose face is it?

Then you may hear what we say only under the bell. The face is not ours. The ancestors carved it, yes — but after a pattern: there was a smaller one on the old stone, and they made it as wide as a valley, so the sky would remember. We call them Those Before the Storms. The storms came when they left.

What were they like?

The pattern-stone has no eyes like ours and no sail. Only a brow, and a ring where the head should be. The ancestors measured the old road by the mesa: one stride of it is nine of ours. And every tale agrees on one thing — while they walked here, the wind was warm. We have counted storms ever since it turned cold.

Why does the face look up?

Because that is where they went. The bell keepers say the face is not looking — it is waiting. A sentry looks down at the road. One who waits looks up. If your tablets say the road runs by the stars, then the face has been turned the right way all along, and it was we who looked at our feet.

What is the ring of opal crystals?

The Opal Crown — our calendar of storms. Eight crystals for eight winds, each leaning toward its own. When a storm is gathering, the crystal on its side glows at night, brighter than opal should. The wardens read it the way you read a page. In the pit in the middle there is only a barrel: the bareskins thought it was a treasury.

And the stone arch and the tall pillar in the canyons?

Wind work. The wind eats the soft dark layers and leaves the hard pale ones hanging: give it a million years, and a ridge becomes an arch, a plateau becomes a tooth. On top of the tallest tooth there is fire opal. At night it shines like a lamp, and travelers steer by it.

What is the six-wheeled iron beast by the dune?

A bareskin animal. It came crawling over the plain on six round legs, very slowly, looking at stones. At the dark dune it sank and lost a leg. We sang over it, as one does over the dead, and left it to the wind. Its tracks are still sharp. Only storms erase anything here, and even they seem to respect it.

What is the iron tower on the ice cap?

The bareskins came for water and drilled into the smoking ice. Smoking ice has no water in it — every ice miner knows that. They brought up salt and white smoke, barrel after barrel, and never went deeper. True ice lies under the false. They were three days of cutting away from it when they left.

Tell me about the storms.

Storms are our years. We say "three storms ago", not "three years". A small storm comes every few days and passes; a great one comes rarely and takes the sky away for days. We are the people who stay through them. Everything else here hides or runs.

What does the sail on your back feel?

Wind before it arrives. The dust carries a tingle ahead of a storm, and the membranes catch it: my sail itches on the side the storm will come from. When it comes, we fold the sail flat, or the wind would take us with it. A young one with an open sail in a storm is a kite.
