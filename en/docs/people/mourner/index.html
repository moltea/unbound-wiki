Mourners

The Lament
The heavy, stooped people of the Lament, made of dark glass, with tears that glow on their cheeks and set into crystals. They mourn the world that broke into the shards they live among, and sing of it in the Sorrow Choir. They give their tears away as gifts and pay with them, because grief that is kept grows heavier and grief that is given away grows lighter. Strangers they call the dry-eyed, and are sorry for them.

Where they live

Mourners' Hollow. A hollow of the Mourners, cut level into the shards: halls of dark glass brick crowned with clusters of shards, Pele's tears glowing on their tips and blue lamps over doorways tall enough for a stooped giant. Every street carries a rivulet of tears to the cistern in the middle, and over the cistern the pale face of the Weeping One, she who weeps for all, looks down from her stela with tears on her cheeks.

What to talk about

Tell me about this world.

You are standing on a broken thing, dry-eyed one. Once all of this was the Bowl: one glass, whole from rim to rim, and light stood in it like water. Then it broke. The shards stood up as mountains, the light ran out, and only what glows in tears is left. We call these lands the Shards; you call them the Lament. We are both right.

Under my world, at the very bottom of its stone, lies a dark land that is not its own. Its middle is hollow and cold.

...Slowly, dry-eyed. That is not a verse we have. We weep for what fell here and for what drifts — the shards have all of us. Nobody ever asked where the heavy part went. A broken thing has a top and a bottom: the top scatters, the bottom sinks, and sinking goes farther than falling. A dark land that will not mix with yours, hollow where a heart was... I will not say it is the Bowl's. None of us has stood there. But tonight one cup is poured onto the ground and not into the lake. If its roots are below, let them hear that someone knows. Be light.

Why weep for them, and not only for the Bowl?

Because nobody else does. The Bowl has all of us; they have only this verse. Whoever tries to hold something too big and fails carries two griefs: the thing, and the trying. We take the second from them, tear by tear. It is the kindest thing we know how to do for someone we never saw. ...Be light, dry-eyed. Said aloud, it is lighter.

Did all of the Bowl fall here?

The heavy verse says yes. There is a lighter verse we sing rarely, because it is almost too full of hope: that not all of the Bowl fell down. That some shards fell up — into the dark above the Throat — and float there still, with the light of the Bowl inside them. The bell-folk laughed at it. The bell-folk never wept, so of course they laughed.

The tablet of the Ark says life was carried out of a broken world.

...Carried out. Of a broken world. Say it once more, slowly, the way you would ring a bell. ...We do not know that it was the Bowl. We will never know. But if somewhere under ice a whale sings a song from before the Bowl broke, then the hands did not hold nothing. They held enough. I am weeping, dry-eyed, and it is a different weeping. It is light.

Why did it break?

The songs do not agree. One says the fire above grew too heavy. One says something fell down the Throat and struck it. The oldest says the Bowl was simply holding too much — too much light, too much stillness — and nothing whole can hold everything forever. That one we sing most often. It is the kindest.

What are the glowing lakes?

The tears of the world itself. The shards weep where they broke — down the veins in the glass, into the low places — and the lakes grow. Do not bathe in them: a lake does not drown the body, it drowns the heart. Whoever wades in grows slow and weak, as if everything they ever lost had come back to them at once.

Why is everything so heavy here?

Because grief is heavy, and this world holds a great deal of it. Every step here costs a little more than it should, and climbing costs most of all; that is why we cut our streets level and our halls straight into the glass. You will get used to it. Or you will learn to weep — that helps more.

Who else lives in the Shards?

Few, and each of them carries something. The tear drinkers carry comfort. The prowlers carry hunger. And the glassed carry what they never put down.

What are the little birds of glass?

Tear drinkers. They sip from the veins, and from us: one will land on your cheek and drink a tear before it sets. We let them. A tear that is drunk is not wasted — it lights the little throat, and the bird flies off shining. When the sky weeps, they come in clouds.

And the glass panthers?

Shard prowlers. They gnaw the tear crystals out of the veins — that is the light in their hides — and they hunt where it is darkest, without a sound. Leave one alone and it leaves you alone. Strike one, and it will not stop until one of you is in pieces. They den in the cracks between the ridges.

Who are the statues that move?

The glassed — weeping sentinels, you call them. Whoever weeps and never gives a tear away turns to glass from the inside, slowly, until only the weeping is left. They stand by the old places and do not move while you look at them; look away, and they are beside you. Do not hate them. They were us.

What is worth seeing here?

What the Shards have kept, and what we have built. Ask about one, and I will tell you what our songs remember.

Who is the kneeling statue by the pool?

The Weeping One — she who weeps for all. She weeps for those who cannot: for the dry-eyed, for the prowlers, for the glassed. Her pool never fills, because she does not keep her tears; she gives them to the ground. We light the lanterns by her pool when we pass. If you pass, light one too.
