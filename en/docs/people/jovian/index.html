Belt Riders

Jupiter
The Belt Riders are living balloons of Jupiter's cloud tops who have never touched anything solid. They once drifted along the wind belts, until the lightning that feeds them made them heavy; now their gondolas are moored to masts that drink the storm, and lightning is their food, their feast and their measure of age. A guest who cannot float at all seems to them impossibly heavy, and so impossibly old, and they greet such a guest with deep, humming respect.

Where they live

Mooring Knot. A knot of the Belt Riders: open gondolas moored to one another and to masts that drink the storm, floating in pools of cream cloud above the cloud tops. Over the Great Mast in the middle a caught lightning burns in a copper cage, and the elder, the heaviest of the knot, hovers beneath it.

What to talk about

Tell me about Jupiter.

This is the Sky, and there is nothing else here. No floor, no bottom — only clouds on top of clouds: the higher, the colder and whiter; the lower, the warmer and darker. Under them lies the Depths, where the air thickens until it turns to metal. We live on the very top, where the lightning reaches up to us.

What is down in the Depths?

Weight. Everything heavy goes there: hail, old gondolas, and in the end, us. The deeper you sink, the harder the air squeezes and the warmer it gets — and the lightning is born down there, in the water clouds, and rises to us. We give the Depths our old ones, and it gives us lightning back. A fair trade.

Why is the sky striped?

Because it breathes. In the white stripes — we call them Rises — the air goes up and the ammonia freezes into white clouds; in the dark ones, the Sinks, it goes down and drags the brown stuff of the Depths with it. Between them the winds run, east and west, faster than any of us can swim. Our ancestors rode them around the whole Sky — that is where our name comes from.

What are those moons in the sky?

The Four. They are moored to the Sky on ropes we cannot see and go round and round, the way gondolas go round a mast in the wind. The yellow one is Ember: she smokes, and her smoke falls to us at the poles as curtains of light. The white one is Icelet, the big one is the Elder, and the dark, pocked one is Speckle. We count our years by them, and our children by the storms.

Who else lives in the Sky?

The floaters — little striped bubbles with big eyes. They are not our kind; they are more like your birds, curious and silly. The storm rays glide on the thunderheads and drink lightning through their wings: rivals at every feast, but honest ones. And in the Spot hunts the nautilus, a shell made of a coiled storm. Whatever the wind carries into the Spot goes down its funnel. Many young ones never came back from the Spot.

Who are you?

We are the Belt Riders. Once all of us rode the winds between the stripes, from Rise to Sink and around the whole Sky, and we needed no masts. But every lightning that feeds us leaves a little weight behind. The old ones grew heavy, the young ones would not leave them — and so we moored. Now our knots hang where the storms pass, and the wind rides by without us.

Why do you call me heavy?

Because you are! Weight is age here: every strike leaves a scar and a little copper in us, and old ones like me can't rise above the masts any more. So the heavier, the more respected. And you can't rise at all — I took you for someone older than the Spot! Though the heavy folk who came before you were all like that. Is your world full of walkers? A whole world of heavy ones... How sad. And how venerable.

Where do young Belt Riders come from?

From bellblooms. Have you seen them on the clouds — little bells of ice on threads, with a spark inside? A bellbloom waits for lightning. Most wait in vain and stay flowers. But if the lightning chooses one, the spark wakes, the threads snap, and a Belt Rider rises — light, light as a breath. That is why we never pick bellblooms. And please, don't put them in your soup.

Why do you have so many eyes?

To see the wind, of course. A storm doesn't come from in front — it comes from everywhere at once. With ten eyes around my belt I see the whole sky except straight up and straight down: up there are only my rods, down there only the Depths, and I don't care to look at either. Do you really see only forward? Then how do you know when a ray is behind you?

What happens when one of you grows too heavy?

Then they let go. We say "untied". One night, in a strong storm, an old one lets go of the mast and goes down into the Depths, slowly, glowing with every old scar. The others hum until the glow is gone. Then we build a drifting stone for them — a slab of storm cloud with a ring on it — and give it to the wind. The wind takes it apart stone by stone, and when the last stone is gone, the old one has reached the bottom.

A lightning that walked?

That is the closest word we have. The old ones who saw it from afar said it had a head — a ring of light on top — and arms held wide, and that it was warm, not burning: you could moor beside it and live. It climbed the invisible ropes to the Four, and the Four still swing on them. Then one day it walked away along a rope nobody can see, and did not come back. Since then we have lived on the little ones.

The tablet from Ember says: the road of fire goes down.

From Ember? Then I know where it went. The old ones said the First Lightning last stood over Ember — and Ember has smoked ever since. We thought she was burning. If your tablet says the road goes down through the fire, she is not burning: she is keeping a door warm. My home was struck the year Ember went dark for a while. Now I wonder what passed through.

The tablet from Icelet says life sleeps under the ice.

Under Icelet? Then that is what hums. On the stillest nights, when no storm is in the Sky, the storm catchers hear a low song from the white one, and we always said it was the ice cracking. Ice does not sing the same song twice, heavy guest. Whatever the First Lightning put to sleep down there is dreaming out loud.

What are those threads on your head?

My rods. They grow from the crown the way, I'm told, hair grows on you — only ours is copper. A spark runs up and down them all the time: that's me thinking. In a storm they stand on end and pull the lightning in. If you see a Belt Rider with drooping rods, give them a jar: they're hungry.
