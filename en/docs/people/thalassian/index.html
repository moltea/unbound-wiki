Thalassians

Thalassia
Amphibious farmers of the tide fields under Pontus, with a crown of amber gills. They sow sea barley where the tide has gone out and reap it before the water comes back; at noon, when Pontus closes the Warm Eye, the fields rest, and so do they. They count time in tides and shadows, read the night glow of Pontus like weather, and call a guest a gardener without a field until the guest has grown something.

The settlement and its dwellers

The chief and the masters

Every settlement has a chief (the elder in its middle) and named masters: names differ from one settlement to the next, the chief wears their own regalia and a crown above the task mark, a master — a star. They say their own lines and give their own tasks.

Chief of the Tide Fields
Elder · Ossaru or Lunei or Hemmo

«Stand in my shadow, one from above — the Eye is fierce today. I am …. When the village has to decide something, it decides it here, under the Mast, at noon, lying down. The others will trade with you. I will tell you how a field is made.»

Where they live

Tidefield Hamlet. A hamlet of the Thalassians on the meadows above the shallows: timber houses of parasol on tidestone plinths, roofs of tidewood and lanternwood, barley beds between the lanes. In the middle stands the Eclipse Mast with blue Pontus in its rings, and at noon, when the giant closes the sun, the whole hamlet rests in its shadow.

What to talk about

Tell me about this world.

A world of tides. The Father of Tides — that great blue one filling half the sky — pulls the sea up the shore and lets it go, every day, and we live in the space between. Below the meadows are the shallows, above them the high flat mountains with walls like cut bread. And everywhere, if you put your ear to the ground, you can hear the water.

Doesn't the tide flood your fields?

Only the low ones, and only the way we want. We cut the furrows so the water runs in and lies there, warm, and runs out again carrying the salt away. A field that the tide never touches goes sour. A field the tide lives in goes to sea. We stand in between, with spades.

Who lives on the flat mountains?

Wind, and birds, and nobody sensible. The walls are bands of red and brown stone, older than tides; at their feet the stone breaks into columns like stacked sheaves. Water falls off their tops after rain. From up there you can see three hamlets at once — that is the only reason to climb.

Who else lives here?

Our herds on the downs, rays in the shallows, terns on the stacks — those you will like. In the parasol forest at night, the pard; in the deep sea, the sawjaw — those you will not. Ask the herder; it is his whole life.

What is that in your sky?

The Father of Tides, the Warm Eye and the Pebble — and us underneath, looking up. Ask about any of them: the sky here is the only clock we have.

What is the Father of Tides?

The Father pulls the sea, and the sea feeds the barley, and the barley feeds us. So he is our father, in a way. He never sets: he only turns his face, thin to full and back. You call him Pontus? It is a good name. Too short for him.

Why does the sun go out at noon?

The Father closes the Warm Eye with his hand, so we can rest. Every noon, all the shadow for a little while, and the fields cool, and we lie down where we are. Children are told that the Eye is shy. The watchers say the Father simply walks in front of it. Both are true.

What is the little moon next to the Father?

The Pebble. Dry, no sea, no tide, nobody home. It runs around the Father faster than anything, and when it crosses his face the watchers make a mark on the post. Some say the Pebble is what the Father throws at storms.

Who are you?

Thalassians, you would say: the people of the tide fields. We walk on land and breathe through our gills when the water comes; we sow at low tide and reap before high. We have no kings — only elders who have counted many tides, and a chief who sits closest to the Mast.

What are the feathers on your heads?

Gills. Three on each side, our crown. On land they rest; in the water they open, and we can stay under for a whole tide. At night the tips glow a little: they drink the light of the Father all day. You can tell how old one of us is by how pale the gills are.

How do you choose your names?

The child is carried to the shore at the first low tide, and the first sound the sea makes going out is the name. That is why our names sound wet to you. A chief gets a second name from the Mast; we do not say it to guests.

What does each of you do?

The tide farmers cut furrows and reap. The herders keep the beasts. The weavers turn sedge into cloth. The shoal gatherers walk the shallows for mats, shells and surf-stone. The eclipse watchers read the Father. And the elders remember. Everyone sits in the shade at noon — that is not a craft, that is life.

Why are some of you shorter?

Young ones. We grow until our gills turn pale, and then we stop and start to remember instead. The short ones run fastest along the lanes when the tide comes — always to the wrong field.

What do you eat?

Barley, of course — grain, bread, porridge with cow's milk. Amberberries, sour as a scolding; they let you stay longer underwater. Eggs from the shellhens. Everything with a little salt. You ones from above eat without salt? How do you know what you are eating?
