Fruiting Folk

The Mycelium
Fruiting bodies of the one mycelium that is the whole world down here. Each walks on four roots for a few flushes and falls, and everything it saw sinks back into the threads, so they say "we" and mean all of it. They do not breathe, and their words come out with spores. They weave light from the glowing threads, beat old threads into felt, light the lantern mushrooms and keep the spores, and they meet every breather who comes down the vent as someone they will remember.

Where they live

Fruiting Ring. A ring of the Fruiting Folk: houses that grew rather than were built — mushrooms with a room in the hollow stalk and glowing gills for a ceiling, hollow stumps hung with bracket fungi — on mounds of felt, joined by walkways of hyphae. In the middle stands the settlement's own elder cap, pale and cracked with light, and under it the elder listens to the Mycelium for everyone.

What to talk about

Tell me about this world.

There is no "this world", breather. There is us. Every growth you walk on, every hollow, every giant cap is one body: the Mycelium. The crimson growths are where it is warm and slow, the warped ones where it is cool and thinking. The light in the hollows is the threads, and the threads are what we think with.

What are the glowing threads?

Our thoughts, more or less. When one of us sees something, light runs along the threads to everyone else, and the brighter a thread, the more is passing through it. Take what you need, but not by the armful: a thread that is dug up keeps its light, but it stops listening.

Why are some growths crimson and others blue?

Two moods of one body. Where the Mycelium is warm and patient it grows crimson, and the titans and the puffs like it there. Where it is cool and quick it grows warped, and the lanterns grow there, because that is where the dark is deepest. Our caps come in both colors and in a third, the ivory of the old ones, who have stopped choosing.

What falls from the sky here?

Our own spores, mostly. Sometimes the growths shake them loose, and they come down like glowing snow; afterwards there is dust on everything. Sometimes the Mycelium breathes out the air the stranglers drank, and for a while even you can breathe without your jar. And sometimes every thread flares at once: then we are thinking together, and anything hiding among the threads is lit up for all to see.

Who else lives here?

The puffs are our breath that tore loose: leave them be, they burst when struck. The croakers are frogs who let us grow on their backs; they catch spores with their tongues and sing to the lanterns. And the stranglers... they lie in the hollows looking like threads. They are threads. Hers. They drink air, and you carry air. Walk on the growths, not in the folds.

What is above the Mycelium?

The Airhole, a long dry throat, and at the top of it the Dry Red, where our mute cousins grow: crimson and warped forests that never learned to remember. We sent spores up once. They came back down on a bell, coughing. Breathers say there is a blue country above even that, where water falls on everything and the dead rot in no time. It sounds like a wonderful place to grow.

Who are you?

We are the fruit. The Mycelium is the tree, if you like, and we are what it grows when it wants to walk about and look at things. Each of us lasts a few flushes, then falls, and what it saw soaks back into the threads. So when you ask who we are, the true answer is "all of it". The short answer is "this cap".

Then why do you have names?

For you. Breathers get muddled when everyone answers to "we". A body is named after the thought the threads were thinking when it pushed out of the ground: this one came up while we were thinking of a spore that would not land. The names are short because the bodies are.

What happens when one of you falls?

It lies down on a growth, the threads come up around it, and a flush or two later there is felt where it lay and nothing missing anywhere. Its last look is the brightest light the threads ever carry. You breathers fall alone, don't you? And everything you saw goes... nowhere? That is the saddest thing we have ever learned.

Don't you need to breathe?

No. Your jar of air looks to us like carrying a little lake on your back in case you get thirsty. We drink through our roots, and what little we need from the air, the threads take from the spores. When we speak, spores come out with the words, and where a word lands, a thread may grow that remembers it. So be careful what you say near us.

What does each of you do?

Thread weavers weave light out of the threads that are still thinking. Felt makers beat the old ones into felt. Spore keepers keep the maybes until they are ready to be scattered. Lamplighters remind the lanterns to shine. Cap cooks feed the sprouts and the guests. And the elder listens for all of us. You can tell a craft from across a hollow: the threads dress us for our work.

Why are some of you blue and some pale?

The blue ones have only just opened: see the torn veil still hanging from their rims? The crimson ones are in the middle of their flushes, with a little one budding at the edge of the cap. The pale, cracked ones are old, and the light gets out through their cracks, the way it does from the Elder Cap. The old remember the most, because they have been listening the longest.

What do you eat?

Nothing, the way you mean it: the roots drink and the threads share. But a sprout just out of the ground has no roots worth the name, so the cooks feed it cap soup, mute caps simmered soft with a pinch of spores, until it can drink from the threads. You breathers eat mushrooms too, don't you? Our mute cousins. We don't mind: they remember nothing. Here, take some pulpcap. It mends wounds.

How did they fall?

They did not fall, really. That is a breathers' word. They lay down — slowly, on purpose, the way a tired body lies down after giving everything away. And then the part this cap does not like: their warmth did not go down into the ground, as a body's warmth should. It went up. Pulled. As if something far above were drinking it. We kept only what soaked in first.
