Gilded

The Forge
Squat smiths of the Forge who are cast rather than born: living gold poured into a shell of slag, with a spark breathed into the mouth. They forge living gold while it still moves, on anvils of slag, and sleep behind walls thick enough to keep out the glow of the sea. A family wears its gold on its shoulders and keeps the rest on its roof, because that gold is its children to come; humans, to them, are "the unforged": soft, cold, and touchingly afraid of the sea.

Where they live

Hearthyards of the Gilded. A settlement of the Gilded: anvil-shaped houses with walls two bricks thick, each with its forge smoking in the yard and the family's gold piled on the roof where every neighbor can count it. In the middle lies the Yard of the Mold, where the Gilded pour their children from family gold and the eldest breathes the first spark into them.

What to talk about

Tell me about this world.

This is the Forge, unforged: the bottom of the Flue, where fire goes when it wants to work. The gold below is the Pour, still deciding what to become. The isles are anvils, slag the Forge struck and left to cool, and the living gold growing on them is work the Forge has finished. We live on the anvils, between the work that's done and the work to come.

What is the golden sea?

The Pour: gold that hasn't cooled since the world was lit. Don't step on it, unforged: it would take your feet and give back slag. We don't step on it either. We were cast from it, not made to swim in it. The little striders run about on it, but they weigh no more than a sigh.

Why does the sea rise and boil sometimes?

That's the Tide: the Forge is filling its ladle. The Pour swells and boils, gold leaps onto the shore, and the striders come running from everywhere to skim the froth. Stay off the edge while it lasts. The splashes don't care whether you're forged or not.

How do you count time without day and night?

In heats. A heat runs from lighting a forge to the moment the gold stops moving. A good smith works two heats and sleeps one. The oldest of us are ten thousand heats old; I'm somewhere in the middle, still hot enough to be foolish. The bell-folk had "days". They kept asking what time it was, as if the Forge cared.

What falls from the sky here?

Embers, when the Forge sneezes: put something on your head, they sting the unforged. Gold mist, when the Pour breathes out: the far isles turn golden and nuggets settle on the roofs, so the children run out with brooms. And the draft: when the Forge draws breath, a hot wind blows across the anvils, and wherever it blows it's hotter still.

What is above the Forge?

The Flue: a long throat of stone, and at the top of it the Red Country, where ash falls and pig-men grunt. That's where the bells come from. The bell-folk said that above the Red Country there's a cold blue one, where water falls from the roof and nothing ever burns. I don't believe it. Who could live where nothing burns?

Why did the sea crust over?

That's the Quench: the Forge dips the world into cold, the way we dip a blade. The Pour skins over with plates of slag, and you can walk far out on them. Salamander crystals set in the crust, and embers bubble up from under it. But when the plates crackle and glow, run for the shore: they melt one by one.

Who are you?

The Gilded. Gold inside, slag outside, and a forge where you keep your teeth. We weren't born, unforged: we were cast. The anvil on our shoulders is our head, the fist is our hammer, and the gold we wear is everything the family owns. That's the short answer; the long one takes a few heats.

What do you mean, cast?

On every yard there's a mold of slag in the shape of one of us. When a family wants a child, it melts down some of the gold off its shoulders and pours it in, and the eldest breathes a spark from its mouth into the gold. The slag skin hardens in one heat, the eyes light in two, and on the third the child gets up and asks for coal. The first thing it gets is a ring on its horn.

Why do you wear so much gold?

Because it's our grandchildren. Every child is poured from the family's gold, so a family wears its gold where anyone can count it: a thin shoulder means a small family to come. What won't fit on the shoulders goes up on the roof. Nobody steals it. Gold that isn't yours won't lie still in your hands; it remembers its own fire.

Your mouth glows a different color as you talk.

It does. Dull red, I'm tired or sad. Cherry, I'm angry; walk away from a cherry smith. Orange is work, yellow is good company, and white is joy, the greatest there is. The bell-folk said that among your kind "white heat" means fury. Strange folk, the unforged: you get angry at the very top.

What do you eat?

Heat: coal every day, charcoal on feast heats, a deep ember when we've been very good. You can't eat any of that, poor cold thing, so here: three firefruits. Hot rind, icy heart. Eat one when the Pour starts to roast you, and for a while you won't feel it.

What does each of you do?

Goldsmiths read the gold and work it fine. Slag masons cut the anvils and build our walls. Crucible keepers watch the melt and pour it. Chainwrights forge chain, the only thing that holds over the Pour. Charcoal burners feed us all. And the eldest keeps the family molds and breathes the first spark. Every craft has its own fire; mix them up and the yard goes cold.

Why are your walls so thick?

For sleeping. We don't sleep the way you do: we cool. We bank the forge in the mouth under ash and lie in the dark, and the slag skin draws tight again. A smith who never cools goes brittle and cracks at the first hard blow. But the Pour shines through anything thinner than two bricks, so our walls are two bricks thick and the windows are lattice.
