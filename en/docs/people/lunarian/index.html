Lunarians

Moon
Tall, slow and quiet, the Lunarians lived on the Moon long before the first rocket. Earth never moves in their sky, so they call it the Still One and count their days by its phases. They grind selenite into lenses better than anyone, and greet whoever brings a telescope as a fellow watcher.

Where they live

Lunar Court. A court of the Lunarians: round domes of dry stone half sunk into the regolith, each with a selenite window on top that looks at the Still One. In the middle lies the Court of the Earth with its basalt mirror, and every path of flat stones leads there.

What to talk about

Tell me about the Moon.

The Moon is a quiet place, and we are quiet with it. Seas of gray stone that never held water, mountains older than any sea, craters left by stones that fell when the sky was young. Nothing here wears away: the dust keeps every footprint for a thousand years.

Why is it so quiet here?

There is no air to carry a sound, guest. A falling rock lands without a word. We talk the way stone does: through the ground, by touch, by the light of the crown. Your suit only pretends to hear me — or perhaps it listens better than you think.

How do you live through the long night?

A day here lasts two of your weeks, and so does the night. When the Sun goes down, the selenite in our crowns gives back the light it drank, and the Still One shines on the maria brighter than your Moon on your fields. We stay close to home, though: the night belongs to the stalkers.

Who else lives on the Moon?

The hoppers graze the maria and listen to Earthlight with their ears; we keep them in pens of low stone. The crater tortoises are older than the seas, and they do not forgive a blow. And at night the selenite stalkers rise from the dust and hunt without a sound. Respect all three and you will live long here.

What is worth seeing around here?

Many things, if you walk slowly. Our ancestors left their marks across the maria, the guests left theirs, and the Moon left the strangest of all. Ask, and I will tell you what the dust has told me.

And the signs cut by the slab — can you read them?

Read them? No. Remember a little? Yes. The Slab-Setters left their marks over the way into the square, on the altar stone, over the narrow door in the north step — that one we do not wake — and by the ring of stones that sleeps in the maria. Our grandmothers knew a few signs, the way you know a constellation without the names of its stars. Ask, and I will tell you what is left.

Which sign is the ring?

The one that looks like the ring of stones itself: a stone frame without corners, hanging from a beam. If something is drawn inside such a frame, that is a name — the name of a world. The Slab-Setters signed every ring with the name of the world it stands on.

And the tall figure?

With a ring for a head and arms spread wide, taller than anything near it? That is them — the Slab-Setters themselves. We draw them the same way, and we never draw their faces.

Is there a sign your people use?

One. Two brackets around a dot — "keep". Our lamplighters cut it over their doors: we keep the light through the long night. My grandmother said the Slab-Setters cut it on the stone of our court before there was a court.

How do you know where a phrase ends?

By the foot. A sign that stands on a little step is something that is done — set, sleep, keep. It always comes last, like the top stone of a cairn: after it, nothing more is laid.

Who were the Slab-Setters?

None of us ever saw them: the slab stood there when the first Lunarian looked up. But under the dust by the slab lie old steps, and they are not ours: each comes up to my chest, and I am not short. And in the oldest songs the long night was shorter and warmer while they were here. Then they went — upwards, the songs say — and the night became the one you know.

Why a slab — and why here?

The Earth Keeper says the slab is not a stone but a finger: it does not point at itself. For a thousand long nights we looked at the slab — and we should have looked where it looks. Here nothing hides the sky: no air, no cloud. If someone wanted to leave a mark for those who come by the sky, there is no better place under the Still One.

The tablet says the road goes by the stars.

Then I owe the Earth Keeper a bowl of water: he said so, and I laughed. We do not leave the Moon — our road ends where the dust ends. But you have ships. Follow the slab's gaze, watcher, and when you come back, tell the court what the Slab-Setters were looking at. We have waited long enough to hear it.

And the line of cairns in the highlands?

The Cairn Trail. The first walkers raised them to find the way back: seven cairns, each taller than the last. At the end there is a hut of dry stone, for the one who could not find the way. One of us reached it and stayed there. We still keep a light on that cairn.
